## What was tested
One full pre-registered Discovery scan: does the hour following the
London FX market's opening bell (roughly 3-4am New York time, when the
world's largest currency-trading center opens for business) show
unusually large price swings in 6E (Euro futures), compared to a normal
hour of trading? The idea: London dealers open with a backlog of
overnight orders that all have to clear against whatever liquidity
exists in that first hour -- no news event involved, just the structure
of the trading day itself.

## Result and why it matters
Yes, and cleanly. The London-open hour moves credibly more than a typical
hour (by about 0.04 "ATR" -- a measure of the day's normal range -- with
a confidence interval that stays entirely on the positive side). Two
things confirm this is the real thing, not a fluke: the hour right
before London opens does NOT show the same elevation (so it's specific
to the session boundary itself, not just "overnight is always noisy"),
and the effect isn't just one thin-liquidity print in the first minute.
This is the first clean, unambiguous pass in the "does volatility spike
at a scheduled or structural boundary" family this project has run --
the two prior entries in this family (an ECB rate decision, an EIA oil
report) either passed with no strategy to attach it to, exactly like
this one. The honest catch: knowing volatility is elevated isn't the
same as having a strategy that profits from it, and 6E has zero existing
strategies to size against this fact. Since this one fires every single
trading day (not a handful of times a year like the other two), it's
different enough to be worth a real standalone strategy design -- but
building and testing that properly is its own separate piece of work,
not something to rush through in the same cycle.
Separately, one new idea was sourced to keep the pipeline stocked:
retesting an old, already-failed pattern (a "stop hunt then reversal"
setup that was tested six different ways months ago and never worked on
average) but this time only on days that follow an already-quiet,
tightly-range-bound session -- using a fact this project already proved
true for a different reason. This came directly out of a conversation
